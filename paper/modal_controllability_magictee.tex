\title{Symmetry-Protected Modal Controllability for Broadband X-Band Magic-T Power Combining}
\author{Author~Names~Omitted~for~Draft}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A broadband 9--11.5~GHz Magic-T junction is analyzed from the viewpoint of modal controllability rather than scalar S-parameter optimization.
Mirror symmetry separates the collinear excitation into even and odd parity channels, which couple ideally to the H and E arms, respectively.
This parity decomposition makes it possible to ask a more informative design question: whether distinct geometric perturbations generate independent directions in the full-band electromagnetic response.
A real Jacobian is constructed from the complex even- and odd-mode reflection trajectories sampled over the complete band and is analyzed by singular values, modal selectivity ratios, and inter-column angles.
For the evaluated stepped-post geometry, the step-normalized full-band Jacobian has singular values approximately $[0.998,0.378,0.234,0.0198]$ and condition number 50.3.
The lower- and upper-post radius response directions are separated by approximately $78.9^\circ$ in the full response space and by $67.6^\circ$ in the odd/E block, demonstrating that the stepped post introduces a strong non-redundant modal-control direction.
The total post height is strongly even selective, with an even-to-odd sensitivity ratio of approximately 17.4.
The same geometry gives mean single-cell H/sum and E/difference power-transfer efficiencies of approximately 87.0\% and 77.5\%, while forbidden parity conversion remains below about $-47$~dB.
These results indicate that the modal architecture is well organized and that the remaining weak band-edge points are primarily a spectral-shaping problem rather than a failure of the underlying Magic-T symmetry or stepped-post concept.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Magic-T, waveguide combiner, even-odd modes, S-parameter sensitivity, Jacobian conditioning, broadband matching, modal controllability.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Broadband waveguide power combiners must satisfy two requirements that are often mixed together in conventional optimization: the junction must preserve the desired modal topology, and its impedance response must remain favorable across frequency.
For a Magic-T, these two requirements are naturally related to parity.
The symmetric and antisymmetric combinations of the two collinear ports form the sum and difference channels, which ideally couple to the H and E arms.
Once this symmetry is respected, poor performance at an isolated frequency does not necessarily mean that the modal architecture is wrong; it may instead indicate insufficient freedom to shape the frequency dependence of the two allowed parity channels.

Most practical broadband Magic-T designs use several geometrically distinct matching elements, such as posts, irises, height steps, septa, or ridge transitions \cite{peng2024ridge,leal2013compact,ggw2022}.
However, simply increasing the number of geometric parameters does not guarantee a useful design space.
If several parameters produce nearly the same electromagnetic response, the optimization problem remains effectively low dimensional and can be poorly conditioned.

The purpose of this work is therefore not to rank geometries by a single worst-case return-loss number.
Instead, the geometry is treated as a set of control directions acting on the two parity-resolved reflection trajectories.
The central question is whether the lower post, upper post, split height, and total height produce independent first-order changes in the broadband scattering response.

The paper makes four contributions.
First, the Magic-T is written in its natural parity basis, where the symmetric structure becomes block diagonal.
Second, a full-band real Jacobian is used to quantify local parametric controllability.
Third, the stepped post is shown numerically to create a strong response direction that is not reproducible by a uniform post.
Fourth, the remaining broadband variation is separated conceptually from modal controllability and assigned to spectral shaping by the throat and ridge-transformer sections.

\section{Parity Structure of the Magic-T}
Let ports 1 and 2 denote the collinear arms, port 3 the H arm, and port 4 the E arm.
Define the even and odd collinear excitations
\begin{equation}
c_+=\frac{P_1+P_2}{\sqrt2},\qquad
c_-=\frac{P_1-P_2}{\sqrt2}.
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal P$ be the operator that exchanges the two collinear arms.
For a mirror-symmetric junction,
\begin{equation}
[\mathcal L,\mathcal P]=0,\qquad [S,\mathcal P]=0,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal L$ denotes the frequency-domain Maxwell operator and $S$ is the scattering operator.
The scattering problem therefore decomposes into invariant parity subspaces,
\begin{equation}
S_m\sim(c_+,H)\oplus(c_-,E).
\label{eq:block}
\end{equation}
The even channel couples to the H arm and the odd channel to the E arm, while cross-parity terms vanish in the ideal symmetric problem.

Define the modal reflection coefficients
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_+(f)=S_{m,11}(f),\qquad
\Gamma_-(f)=S_{m,22}(f).
\end{equation}
For the collinear physical ports,
\begin{equation}
S_{11}=\frac{\Gamma_++\Gamma_-}{2},\qquad
S_{12}=\frac{\Gamma_+-\Gamma_-}{2},
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
|S_{11}|^2+|S_{12}|^2
=\frac{|\Gamma_+|^2+|\Gamma_-|^2}{2}.
\end{equation}
This identity shows why physical-port return loss alone can obscure the underlying modal state: cancellation between $\Gamma_+$ and $\Gamma_-$ can reduce one physical-port quantity without implying that both parity channels are individually well matched.

For the evaluated geometry, the largest forbidden parity-conversion term over 9--11.5~GHz is approximately
\begin{equation}
\boxed{-46.99~\mathrm{dB}}.
\end{equation}
This very small leakage is important because it separates two issues that are otherwise easy to confuse.
The symmetry-protected modal topology is already well preserved; the remaining reflection is predominantly mismatch within the allowed parity blocks.

Figure~\ref{fig:gamma-trajectories} shows the corresponding complex modal-reflection trajectories over the complete band.  The purpose of this plot is not to seek a visually small loop by itself, but to make clear that the residual evolution occurs inside the two allowed parity channels.

\section{Full-Band Parametric Controllability}
Let the geometric parameter vector be
\begin{equation}
\bm p=(p_1,\ldots,p_M)^T.
\end{equation}
At $N$ sampled frequencies, define the real full-band response vector
\begin{equation}
\bm F(\bm p)=
[\Re\bm\Gamma_+;\Im\bm\Gamma_+;
 \Re\bm\Gamma_-;\Im\bm\Gamma_-].
\end{equation}
The local geometry-to-response Jacobian is
\begin{equation}
J=\frac{\partial\bm F}{\partial\bm p}
\in\mathbb R^{4N\times M}.
\end{equation}
The even/H and odd/E row blocks are denoted $J_+$ and $J_-$.

Because the parameters are measured on different natural scales, the analysis uses a step-normalized Jacobian
\begin{equation}
\widetilde J=JD,\qquad
D=\mathrm{diag}(\Delta p_1,\ldots,\Delta p_M),
\end{equation}
where $\Delta p_i$ is the central-difference half step.
The singular values of $\widetilde J$ measure the strength of independent broadband response directions, and
\begin{equation}
\kappa(\widetilde J)=
\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\widetilde J)}
{\sigma_{\min}(\widetilde J)}
\end{equation}
measures the anisotropy of the local design space.

For two response columns $j_i$ and $j_k$, the acute angle
\begin{equation}
\theta_{ik}
=\cos^{-1}\!\left(
\frac{|j_i^Tj_k|}{\|j_i\|_2\|j_k\|_2}
\right)
\end{equation}
provides a direct redundancy diagnostic.
Angles near zero indicate nearly interchangeable controls, whereas large angles indicate genuinely distinct electromagnetic actions.

The term ``controllability'' is used here in a local parametric sense rather than in the state-space sense of dynamical systems.
It refers to the first-order response subspace that can be reached by small geometry perturbations.

\section{Why the Stepped Post Is Electromagnetically Different from a Uniform Post}
Let $j_L$ and $j_U$ denote the full-band response columns associated with the lower- and upper-radius perturbations.
A uniform through-post ties the two radii together, so that
\begin{equation}
\delta r_L=\delta r_U=\delta r,
\end{equation}
and the first-order response is restricted to
\begin{equation}
\delta\bm F=(j_L+j_U)\delta r.
\end{equation}
The accessible perturbation space is therefore one dimensional with respect to the two post regions.

A stepped post releases this constraint:
\begin{equation}
\delta\bm F=j_L\delta r_L+j_U\delta r_U.
\label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
If $j_L$ and $j_U$ are not collinear, the reachable response set expands from a line to a two-dimensional local subspace.

The full-wave identification gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\angle(j_L,j_U)\approx78.9^\circ}.
\end{equation}
Within the parity blocks,
\begin{equation}
\angle(j_{L,+},j_{U,+})\approx65.9^\circ,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\angle(j_{L,-},j_{U,-})\approx67.6^\circ}.
\end{equation}
These large angles show that the two post regions are not merely redundant tuning knobs.
They act on the broadband modal response in substantially different directions.
Figure~\ref{fig:angle-matrix} places this result in the context of all four local controls and makes the remaining near-redundancies immediately visible.

\input{fig_modal_angle_matrix.tex}

The conclusion is also supported by sensitivity magnitude.
Per millimeter of perturbation,
\begin{align}
\|j_L\|_2&\approx1.805,\\
\|j_U\|_2&\approx2.455.
\end{align}
The upper radius is therefore not a weak trim parameter; it is one of the strongest local controls in the design.

The physical origin can be understood using first-order shape sensitivity.
For a normal boundary displacement $V_n$, an S-parameter variation has an adjoint/Hadamard form \cite{zhu2009,dadash2014},
\begin{equation}
\delta S=
\int_{\partial\Omega}
G_S(\bm r,\omega)V_n(\bm r)\,dS.
\end{equation}
The lower and upper post surfaces occupy different regions of the junction field.
Because the even/H and odd/E fields have different parity and spatial localization, the corresponding overlap integrals are not expected to be proportional.
The measured response angles are the numerical signature of this spatially distinct field overlap.

\section{Modal Roles of the Four Geometric Controls}
The complete step-normalized singular spectrum is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\sigma(\widetilde J)
\approx[0.998,0.378,0.234,0.0198]
}.
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\kappa(\widetilde J)\approx50.3}.
\end{equation}
The even/H and odd/E blocks have condition numbers
\begin{equation}
\kappa(\widetilde J_+)\approx67.6,\qquad
\kappa(\widetilde J_-)\approx44.7.
\end{equation}

These values do not mean that the design lacks modal freedom.
Three singular directions are comparatively strong, while one combination is weak.
The normalized weakest full-space right-singular vector is approximately
\begin{equation}
0.47\,\delta r_L
-1.00\,\delta h_s
+0.22\,\delta r_U
+0.085\,\delta H.
\end{equation}
The weak response is therefore dominated by a cancellation involving split height rather than by the absence of a post-control direction.
Figure~\ref{fig:singular-spectrum} shows the complete singular spectra of the full, even/H, and odd/E Jacobians.  The logarithmic scale emphasizes the separation between the strong controllable directions and the single weak combination.

The total height $H$ is particularly useful because it is strongly even selective.
An even more revealing comparison is obtained by contrasting $H$ with the upper radius:
\begin{equation}
\angle(j_{U,+},j_{H,+})\approx11.9^\circ,
\end{equation}
whereas
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\angle(j_{U,-},j_{H,-})\approx82.4^\circ}.
\end{equation}
Thus two geometric changes that look almost interchangeable from the even/H channel become nearly orthogonal when viewed from the odd/E channel.
This is precisely the type of structure that is lost in a scalar physical-port optimization.

The worst modal return-loss values are approximately 6.58~dB for the even/H block and 5.28~dB for the odd/E block.
Those worst points are clearly not as strong as the mean transfer performance.
However, the combination of substantial mean transfer and roughly $-47$~dB forbidden parity leakage gives a more specific diagnosis than a single pass/fail threshold.
The cell is coupling power into the intended parity channels over most of the band, while the weakest frequencies are associated with residual in-block mismatch rather than symmetry breakdown.

This distinction matters because the corrective action is different.
If parity leakage were large, the geometry or topology itself would need to be reconsidered.
When parity leakage is already extremely small, the more natural next step is to reshape the frequency dependence of the allowed modal reflections.

\section{Modal Controllability Versus Spectral Shaping}
Independent modal controls do not automatically provide arbitrary broadband flattening.
Over the 24.4\% fractional bandwidth considered here, each modal reflection can be expanded locally as
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_\pm(f)=
\Gamma_\pm(f_0)
+\Gamma_\pm'(f_0)(f-f_0)
+\frac12\Gamma_\pm''(f_0)(f-f_0)^2+\cdots.
\end{equation}
A local post mainly changes reactive loading and field overlap near the junction.
In contrast, a throat or distributed ridge transformer modifies both the local impedance trajectory and the accumulated propagation phase.
A first-order distributed-reflection picture is
\begin{equation}
\Gamma(f)\approx
\frac12\int
\frac{d\ln Z(z)}{dz}
\exp\!\left[
-2j\int_0^z\beta(\xi,f)d\xi
\right]dz.
\end{equation}
This motivates a two-layer interpretation of the architecture:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\text{local stepped geometry}\rightarrow\text{modal controllability},
\qquad
\text{throat/ridge distribution}\rightarrow\text{spectral shaping}.
}
\end{equation}

Under this interpretation, the stepped post has already accomplished its principal task: it provides several independent directions with which the two parity blocks can be moved.
The remaining weak band-edge frequencies should therefore be attacked mainly through parameters that alter the frequency dependence of those responses, rather than by repeatedly adding more local post variables.

\section{Implication for a Four-Way Combiner}
For four coherent equal inputs,
\begin{equation}
\bm a_\Sigma=\frac12[1,1,1,1]^T,
\end{equation}
the desired state is the global sum mode.
A two-stage binary Magic-T tree performs a hierarchical sum/difference transform: pairwise difference channels ideally vanish in the first stage, while pairwise sums are combined in the second.

In the present PEC model, the complete four-way tree has a mean combining efficiency of approximately 78.34\%, and the network-model versus full-3-D RMS efficiency discrepancy is approximately 1.22 percentage points.
The close agreement between the reduced network representation and the full 3-D model supports the interpretation that the tree topology and coherent phasing are basically correct.
The dominant remaining limitation is therefore the frequency-dependent matching of the constituent cells rather than a breakdown of the binary combining principle.

\section{Discussion}
The most important result is not that one particular geometry has a certain S-parameter value.
It is that the geometry of the local design space can be measured and interpreted physically.
An earlier parameterization exhibited a step-normalized condition number of approximately 422, indicating that several nominal controls produced nearly redundant electromagnetic responses.
The stepped-post geometry studied here is qualitatively different.
The lower and upper radii are strong and widely separated controls, while the total height provides a highly even-selective direction.
The remaining poor conditioning is associated with one cancellation-dominated parameter combination, not with a collapse of the entire modal-control space.

This viewpoint also clarifies why average performance and worst-case performance should not be conflated.
A few weak frequencies matter for a final engineering specification, but they do not by themselves invalidate the modal design concept.
When the mean transfer is already useful and cross-parity leakage is extremely small, the physically relevant interpretation is that the modal basis is correct and the residual task is spectral flattening.

The present study is based on PEC full-wave simulations.
It does not establish conductor loss, thermal margin, breakdown, multipactor threshold, or final peak-power capability.
Those are separate validation problems that must be addressed before a hardware power rating can be claimed.

\section{Conclusion}
A broadband X-band Magic-T was analyzed in the natural parity basis using a full-band geometry-to-scattering Jacobian.
The stepped-post architecture was shown to provide a genuine increase in local modal-control freedom: the lower- and upper-radius response directions are separated by approximately $78.9^\circ$ in the full response space and $67.6^\circ$ in the odd/E block.
The total height is strongly even selective, with an even-to-odd sensitivity ratio of approximately 17.4.

The evaluated cell provides mean H/sum and E/difference transfer efficiencies of approximately 87.0\% and 77.5\%, respectively, while forbidden parity conversion remains below about $-47$~dB.
These results support the interpretation that the underlying modal architecture is functioning as intended.
The remaining weak frequencies are better viewed as a spectral-shaping limitation than as a failure of symmetry or of the stepped-post mechanism.

The design principle emerging from the analysis is therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\text{first create independent modal controls, then shape their spectra}.}
\end{equation}
This principle provides a systematic alternative to treating every geometric parameter as an undifferentiated variable in a scalar broadband optimization.
